## Nicodemus and the Messiah

Nicodemus was a Pharisee. He was waiting for the Messiah for a long time. The Pharisees were a group of very religious lay people in Israel. They were not priests or Levites. They were people who took their faith very seriously. For a lot of them, Nicodemus included, that meant expecting and waiting on the anointed one of God — the Messiah.

If you grew up in the church, you might conflate the terms “Messiah” and “Son of God,” but it’s an important distinction. In the Hebrew scriptures, there’s nothing inherently divine about the messianic texts. The Messiah is a person anointed by God, but not God. A scriptural innovation that Christians make is claiming that Jesus is both the Messiah and the Son of God.

Nicodemus was waiting for the Messiah, and he noticed things about Jesus. He noticed the teachings of Jesus and the miracles of Jesus. He had been waiting for something new to take place, and so he left what was comfortable for him and went to see Jesus. He came by night, so others couldn’t see him. He left what was comfortable in order to see Jesus. And he paid attention to the words of Jesus. He listened to them. He listened to how strange they were.

## Taking the Bible Seriously

There’s been a lot of conflict within the church over the last hundred, two hundred years over biblical literalism. Different traditions talk about reading the Bible literally. That’s where it comes from — from the Latin littera, which means “the letter.” If you go back to the medieval church, they had a fourfold way of reading the Bible. The first was the literal way — that is, what do the letters say? What do they mean? The next was the allegorical — what is the symbol going on? What is the deeper meaning? The next was the tropological or moral — what is a moral teaching we can draw out of the scriptural text? And finally, the anagogical or eschatological — how does this point to the last things, or give us an understanding of our last things with God?

One of the things Luther did was kind of cut off the last three, so it was focused just on the literal: what are the words themselves saying? But he and others have to deal with the fact that Jesus uses figurative language a lot. The Bible is filled with figurative language.

So how do you take figurative language literally? “If you have faith the size of a mustard seed” — well, what size is faith? Is faith an object that has size or volume or takes up space? Is there a mass to faith? That’s taking it literally. But that’s not what people mean when they say to take the Bible literally. They don’t mean that. Obviously. But it’s these “obviously’s” that are built on ideologies, and that’s where the conflict comes in. It’s the assumptions that people make in their traditions that lead to conflict.

For most of Christian tradition, Christians have taken seriously — I use “seriously” more than “literally” — seriously, those texts which align with their ideology, and held lightly those that don’t. There are traditions that take deeply the language of the Bible around marriage but hold lightly language in the Bible around usury. They take seriously language of the Bible around authority, like in Romans 13, and hold lightly language in the Bible around being children of the kingdom of heaven, having no father or master.

## Born Again

All this gets us back to Nicodemus, who took the words of Jesus seriously. He was challenged when Jesus said, “You must be born again,” because birth was what happens at the beginning of your life. How can I be born again? Must I go back into my mother’s belly? He came from where he was comfortable into a new country, and he was challenged by it. But he was willing to listen and confront it, and in that he was transformed — and gave Jesus the opportunity to share the fullness of his good news in John 3:16.

Abraham went from his country, Ur of the Chaldees, to a new land on the word of God, by faith. He was willing to go from the comfortable to the uncomfortable out of obedience to the God of all creation.

This is what it means to take the Bible seriously. Karl Barth calls it “the strange new world of the Bible.” If you’re reading the Bible and it’s not very strange, I think you need to read it differently. If it’s not very new, you need to read it differently. If it doesn’t look like a completely different world, you need to read it differently.

There’s this distinction scholars make between eisegesis and exegesis. Eisegesis is reading into the text. It’s taking our assumptions and expectations and putting them there in the Bible to give ourselves confirmation about those assumptions and expectations. We read the Bible for the precise purpose of confirmation bias, so that we have a source to confirm how we wanted to live anyway. Exegesis is reading out of the Bible. It’s God speaking to us. And what that means is change. What that means for us is change.

Nicodemus left where he was comfortable and went to see Jesus. For us, this is what it means to read deeply, to spend time with scripture. It means leaving a place that is comfortable in order to meet and see Jesus.

## Coming to the Table

This is what it means to come to the table at communion. It means leaving someplace that’s comfortable to do something that is uncomfortable. Let’s put on our Nicodemus hats and come to the table.

Wait — you’re eating the body of Jesus? Are you cannibals? You’re drinking the blood of Jesus? Are you psychos? There’s a response, you know, that some Christians could say: “Oh, don’t take it so seriously. It’s just what we do.” When in fact the faithful response is to take it more seriously. Yes, we are eating the body of Jesus. Yes, we are drinking the blood of Jesus. Jesus is here. Our God, who created everything, is here. The one who sacrificed himself for us is here and gives himself to us.

We receive an amazing gift this day, one that is given freely, so we can receive it in a lot of different ways. We can receive lightly or seriously. We can come to the table solemnly, reflecting on the miracle of life given to us — that we are allowed to participate in the remaking of this world, that we are given a foretaste of the coming kingdom of heaven. It is something concrete that we can bite and chew and swallow.

And God gives us the freedom to just go through the motions. But why? Why waste this opportunity? The strange new world of the Lord’s table, offered for you. Coming to see Jesus, to touch Jesus, to taste Jesus.

## The Strange New World

This is what happens when we open our Bibles. It doesn’t always happen in the easiest of ways. There are a lot of verses of the Bible that you won’t find on a sign at Hobby Lobby to put in your kitchen. We have to wrestle with that. Be unsettled with that.

Martin Luther called the Bible the swaddling clothes in which we find the baby Jesus. The swaddling clothes were dirty. When a baby is born, it’s really messy. There’s lots of fluids and blood. But it holds Jesus.

In this season of Lent, may you be willing to go from your country to meet Jesus in a new way — in the Bible, at the table, in your neighbor, even in your enemy. God is here for you.

In the name of the Father, and the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Amen.
