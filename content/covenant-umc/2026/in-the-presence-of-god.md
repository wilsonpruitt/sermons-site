# In the Presence of God

What does it mean to be in the presence of something beyond ourselves—when we are in awe, amazed at what is happening, and don’t quite have the words?

Both the Old and New Testaments are filled with moments such as these. In Genesis, we have the moment of Jacob’s ladder, when Jacob, the scheming second son of Isaac who deceives his father and brother, has this vision from heaven. He has another moment where he wrestles with the angel, where he knows he is in a divine presence. We have Moses and the burning bush, where the words of God tell Moses to take off his shoes, for he is standing on holy ground.

The presence of God is found in those moments beyond ourself, where our experience transcends us. It can be on a mountaintop or a quiet in a storm, in the touch of a child, or the comfort of a friend.

How do we deal with the presence of God? How do we remember the presence of God during those seasons when the light shines dimly in our lives, or when our loved ones no longer see the light?

In our reading from Samuel, the prophet is concerned about the direction of the country, and God tells him to go to a strange place in order to anoint the next king. Samuel, who has dedicated his life to God, who speaks with God, is still concerned and perplexed. He doesn’t know what is going to happen. And the presence of God is revealed in the most surprising of ways. The youngest son—the most insignificant one—is designated as the king, the anointed, the successor who will grow up to be handsome and strong, a great warrior, a poet. But he starts in the most surprising way, in a manner that can only point to God’s glory and the presence of God.

The point is not that God saw the potential of David. God chose David to reveal God’s power. There is a synergistic relationship going on. Samuel doesn’t go to Jesse’s house to try and pick a king based on their potential or who they will grow up to be. Sometimes we read the story and imagine a secular world where there is no supernatural power, no divinity, no spirit that interacts with the world. Instead, it’s kind of like the NFL draft, and we’ve got scouts coming in trying to predict how these boys are going to king in the future.

No—God is a part of the future of David and the transformation that will take place in David’s life. David was chosen to glorify God, to reveal God’s presence to Samuel and to the people.

When we turn to the Gospel of John, we have a feel-good story. John chapter nine is a feel-good story at first glance. But many of the ways in which it has been read have been deeply problematic and dehumanizing to people with disabilities. 

Amy Kenny, in her book, My Body is not a Prayer Request, offers another interpretation. 

At the beginning of John 9, when the disciples of Jesus are debating whether the man born blind is blind because of his sin or his parents’ sin. As Amy Kenny writes, “They are so caught up in prescribing the cause of his disability that we don’t even learn his name. Do they even know it, I wonder?” She names him Zechariah or Zach to humanize him.

This is how she characterizes Jesus’ response to the behavior of the other people towards Zach:

>Jesus is having none of it. Jesus inverts their idea of blindness by showing the disciples that disability becomes a place of encounter with the glory of God. Jesus interacts with Zach directly, talking not just about him, but to him and with him. According to Jesus, Zach’s blindness didn’t result from his or his parents’ sin, but instead his blindness displays God. What a powerful, subversive statement: disability helps reveal the Light of the World to people who think of themselves as holier than disabled people. Disability is no longer a symbol of sin but one of being open to revelation. Disability unveils God’s work to the community, if only people are willing to receive it.

What are our entry points into the presence of God? That’s what this passage is about. And the presence of God may be more difficult to reach as an able-bodied person. 

You may think this reading is a stretch but it follows the original Greek more closely. I translate verse 3 in the following way: Jesus answered, ‘Neither this one sinned, nor his parents, but it was that the works of God should be displayed in him.’

Jesus doesn’t justify suffering. To read it this way is to equate a lack of sight with suffering. God is displayed in the lives of those dismissed by society. 

Earlier in her book, Kenny writes about the moment Jesus tells people to cut off their arm if it sins. Here is the full passage. 

>If your hand causes you to sin, cut it off. It is better for you to enter life crippled than with two hands to go to hell, to the unquenchable fire. And if your foot causes you to sin, cut it off. It is better for you to enter life lame than with two feet to be thrown into hell. And if your eye causes you to sin, tear it out” (Mark 9:43–47 ESV). We get it, Jesus. It’s better to be lame, blind, and crippled than go to hell. It feels a little extreme to perform voluntary amputations to avoid sin. We’d have a lot more folks with amputations in our midst if we took this literally. Yet Jesus still claims that disability becomes a way to encounter God or a preventative remedy for sin, suggesting that being nondisabled might enhance temptation.

This may be a radically different reading than you are used to. Our society constantly others and pities and dismisses those with bodies and minds which are non-standard. This dehumanizing gaze is not based in the Bible or a theological understanding of who God is or how God created us. If we believe everyone was made in the image of God, there is no deficiency to that image based on ability. We don’t earn it. It is gift.

And to be healed as the world sees it, is not to make life automatically better. In John 9, Zach’s life is not better once he sees. People question him continually. Most of the chapter is made up of people questioning him and suspicious of what happened. They don’t see the presence of God. They still see a deficit.

To be in the presence of God takes a journey. It takes a decision and a realization.

And there may be a greater spiritual challenge for the able-bodied than the disabled. Able-bodiedness can easily lead to a self-deception that denies our connection to God and to our community. 

God’s light is not often found where our culture tells us it will be. The story of David illustrates this, as does the story of Zach in John. Jesus is the light of the world but that light shines where Jesus wills, not where we want to see it. The light of Christ comforts the forgotten of society. If we want to be in the presence of God, we need to be willing to leave what is comfortable to go where God already is. 

In this season of Lent, may you take the opportunity to seek the presence of God in new ways. May you let go of the patterns of the dehumanizing gaze of a culture that judges people on assumptions of ability instead of receiving every neighbor as a gift from God. Our neighbors aren’t objects we use to navigate in this world. We aren’t objects in someone else’s life. You matter to God, and so does your neighbor. Whether the world thinks so or not, you matter to God. As we get closer to Holy Week and we reflect on Christ’s great sacrifice for us. May we remember that God didn’t choose us based on what we can do. We were loved before we could do anything. 

In the name of the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Amen.
