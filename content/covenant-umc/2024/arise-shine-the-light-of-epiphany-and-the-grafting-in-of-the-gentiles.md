It corrects popular distortions of the Magi (pagan, not Jewish wise men, number unknown) to argue that Epiphany celebrates the inclusion of the Gentiles in the promise of Abraham. When I was in my twenties, I went to a lot of movies by myself. Part of the reason was that it was convenient. Another reason is that I like to sit at the very front of the movie theater so that all of my peripheral vision is occupied by the screen. 
The third reason was that I'd like to go during matinee. I had flexible hours because of school, so I was able to go in the middle of the day. It was also cheaper. 

One of the striking aspects of going to a matinée is the sense of walking back outside into a sunny place, having our pupils contract so much and so quickly that it can give you a headache. They were fully dilated inside the theater in order to absorb as much light as possible, but there wasn't a lot of light, and then you step out and it's overwhelming. 

Light language covers all of the scriptures from the very beginning, God created light. 
We see in John that Jesus is the light which shines out the darkness. 
The early Christians did not see light as a simple metaphor, but as a conceptual understanding of who God is. The first scientific text in the West on light was by a medieval monk named Robert Grosseteste called Optics. Grosseteste made a major breakthrough in empirical science because of the centrality of light and the scriptural understanding of who God is around light. He wished to take light seriously.

All of that gets us to this day of epiphany. Now, epiphany is one of those Christian holy days that used to be a giant deal before the Reformation. It later drifted away for Protestant churches and cultures. With the rise of the corporatization of Christmas, there became this conflation between the Magi from the East and the Nativity. 
When we're talking scripturally, the term we need to use is Magi. There are no wise men. There are no three. There could have been two or ten or twenty. The number three comes from later traditions. Magi is plural for Magus, or magic user, they could have had a number of different meanings. The most important of which though is that they were Pagan. They were not Jews. The Magi are not Jews, which is what makes their appearance so striking. 
Epiphany marks this celebration where Christ's salvific action is opened up for all peoples, not just the tribe of Abraham. I honestly think one of the ways and reasons why many Protestants do not place much importance on epiphany is that there was often a conflation between Israel and the church. 
This almost one-to-one comparison made it that the church is the new Israel, and so the promise of Israel is now the promise of the church. One of the ways this is described is called Supersessionism, that is, the church supersedes Israel. 
This has led to many dreadful actions by Christians against Jews throughout history. Protestants by no means were the worst culprits in antisemitism. Throughout history, there were pograms against the Jews for centuries. often functioned as there would be a plague in a region and people wanted to blame somebody, so they blamed the Jews. 
The reason why there were so many Jews in Poland before the Holocaust was because they had already been kicked out of Germany in the 13 hundreds. 

But Epiphany offers us an opportunity to look not at super sessionism, but at inclusion. Not a patronizing inclusion which says that Jews are okay for the Christian God, but an inclusion that says that the Gentiles are included in the promise of Abraham. This is because the Magi see in Christ the fulfillment of something spectacular. Paul will talk about this in Romans 12; the reason why he has missions to the Gentiles that they have been grafted on to the tribe of Israel. An agricultural metaphor that our non-agricultural society often misses. My favorite example of grafting is when the Vineyards of France and much of Europe were decimated by phylloxera in the late 19th century, and there was a Texan agronomist, T.V. Munson, who found a wild grape varietal that was resistant to the pest. 
He brought it over to Europe and started grafting on the old world vines to this Texan grape and the the vineyards survived. French wine was saved. The this Texan earned the Ordre du Mérite agricole, the second highest award in France. 
French wines were grafted onto the roots that could save them. The wines stayed themselves. They stayed unique. The Pinot noir grown in the Burgundy Valley did not replace the stalk from Bell County, Texas. 
The stalk did not replace the Pinot noir. Yet together they were healthy.

The light of Epiphany is about this expansion and opening of the promise of Abraham to the world.
The word epiphany means appearance or manifestation. 
The light was so bright for the magi to see because the darkness was so dark in the world. The light was so easy to follow because the darkness was so dark. 
Their pupils had been so dilated that they were ready to see a glimpse of the true light, no matter how far away or how strange it first seemed.
Isaiah sings, arise, shine. For your light has come. There's been an appearance of hope, of peace, of love. This hope, this love, this peace is not ours to possess. It is a gift offered. 
Part of the gift is a way of seeing the world as a promise fulfilled. 
We don't need to see in the darkness anymore. As well, even when it is dark, we know where the true light comes from. 
The light that guides us to our eternal home is in the world thanks to the coming of Jesus Christ. We need to be consistently reminded of this. New years is a great time for this reminder.
John Wesley, the founder of Methodism, would have a special worship surface on New Year's Eve or New Year's Day called a Covenant Service. He would have the people who gathered together make a covenant for the New Year with God. 
A covenant is deeper than a promise. A covenant goes both ways. You can't have a one-sided covenant like you can't have a one-sided marriage. The marriage ceremony doesn’t ask only one partner if they wish to be married. It isn’t valid unless both agree. You both have to say yes to accept the covenant God offers. We see an epiphany of opening up the promise of Abraham to all peoples. We should accept it. We should embrace it to its fullest, and the promise of Jesus Christ is the redemption of our sins, life abundant; salvation. There's nothing greater. Yet, even still, how great it is. It's so easy for humans to try and domesticate it this gift of God in Jesus Christ that shines light in the world so bright that the magi see it from miles away. Domesticate the faith, domesticate the church, make it comfortable and palatable. Turn it into three simple rules, or a comforting daily devotion, or a message that affirms everything about us without asking anything of us. We can set it on our shelf next to the picture of the Longhorns or our kids or the chochki we got in Canton or Round Top, but it's about the transformation of our whole selves. This is what Christ asks of us. This is the light offered to all people. The covenant prayer which John Wesley used helps us to remember that.
In a few moments, we are going say a summary of it together. 
Like anything we say in church, if you don't feel comfortable with the words, you don't have to say them. These are not words to mindlessly repeat. They are words to aspire to. We say them together so that we can aspire as the body of Christ to be the kind of people who follow God in this way. Let us pray.
